Representing the University of Cincinnati in the CFA Institute Research Challenge, I worked with a team of five to produce a 15-page equity research report on Lancaster Colony Corporation (NASDAQ: LANC), presented to a panel of industry professionals.

We found a company that looked safe but not dynamic — a current ratio averaging 2.14, declining retail margins, and a growth story leaning on licensed products (Chick-fil-A sauces, Olive Garden dressings) rather than organic growth. A full DCF alongside comparable company analysis, forecasting five years out, led us to issue a Sell: a target price of $125.30 against a closing price of $192.89 — a 29.8% downside.

Working in a team of six, I valued both companies independently before touching the deal logic, because an acquisition case built on a bad standalone valuation is worthless. For General Mills — 150+ years old, 100+ brands, four segments (North America Retail, International, Pet, North America Foodservice) — I built a five-year DCF: EBIT growing from $3.05B to $3.71B, a 7.28% WACC, 3% terminal growth, arriving at $66.84 per share. For Lancaster Colony, a second DCF at a 6.5% WACC and 2% terminal growth produced $184.45 per share on a firm value of roughly $5.06 billion.

Then the combination. Using a NOPAT/FCFF framework, I modeled the merged company's sales growing from $22.96B to $27.26B over five years, producing a combined firm value of about $57.1 billion and a per-share value of $95.90. I cross-checked both DCFs against market multiples rather than trusting one method, and built the strategic case on top of the numbers: Lancaster's specialty portfolio (Marzetti, New York Bakery, Sister Schubert's) diversifies General Mills' offerings, its retail and foodservice distribution accelerates market penetration, and procurement, manufacturing, and supply-chain integration drives the margin story. A full SWOT and an industry analysis — plant-based growth, sustainability and traceability trends, the casual-dining shift — framed where the combined company wins and where it is exposed. The integration case supports an estimated 13.5% EBITDA increase and roughly $333 million in revenue growth.

I developed the fund in Python (March 2024), screening for stocks that score well across three factors rather than betting on any single one: value to avoid overpaying, quality to avoid value traps, and momentum to avoid catching falling knives. The factors are combined into a single long-only portfolio — no leverage, no shorting — which keeps the strategy honest: if it works, it works because the stock selection works.

The result: over three years the fund returned 12.5%, outperforming the market by 3.2%.

The question: can you build a forecasting model for a real economy — German real GDP, interest rates, and unemployment — that beats a simple benchmark, and can you say what actually drives what? Macro data is unforgiving: series trend, wander, and mislead, so the first stage was stationarity testing on all three variables — a model built on non-stationary data produces confident nonsense. From there, I estimated dynamic regression models linking GDP, interest rates, and unemployment, examined the seasonality in the data (the German economy has strong seasonal rhythms), and ran a Granger causality test to ask the harder question: does one variable actually help predict another, or do they just move together?

Model selection was done the honest way — out-of-sample-style forecast accuracy. My best specification, a dynamic model with seasonal dummies, achieved an RMSE of 2.679 against 2.743 for the average benchmark model: a modest edge, reported as exactly that.
